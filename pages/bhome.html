SUPERPOSITION

Particles exist in multiple states simultaneously until observed.

▾
Superposition is one of quantum mechanics' most mind-bending principles. A quantum particle — like an electron — doesn't have a definite state until it is measured. Instead, it exists as a probability wave across all possible states at once.

The famous thought experiment of Schrödinger's Cat captures this: a cat in a sealed box is simultaneously alive and dead until the box is opened and observed.
⚡ KEY FACT Quantum computers exploit superposition — a qubit can be 0 and 1 simultaneously — enabling massively parallel computation impossible on classical hardware.

DOUBLE SLIT EXPERIMENT

Shows particles behave like waves and collapse upon observation.

▾
When electrons are fired through two slits without observation, they create an interference pattern — as if each electron passes through both slits as a wave simultaneously.

But the moment a detector watches which slit the electron uses, the interference pattern disappears and the electron behaves like a particle. The act of observation collapses the wave function.
⚡ KEY FACT This experiment proved that quantum objects don't have fixed properties until observed — the universe at its smallest scale is fundamentally probabilistic.

ENTANGLEMENT

Two particles remain connected; measuring one instantly affects the other.

▾
Quantum entanglement links two particles so that the quantum state of each cannot be described independently — even across vast distances. Einstein famously called this "spooky action at a distance".

When you measure the spin of one entangled particle and get "up", the other instantly shows "down" — regardless of the distance between them. This has been confirmed experimentally over distances exceeding 1,200 km.
⚡ KEY FACT Entanglement is the backbone of quantum cryptography and quantum teleportation — enabling theoretically unbreakable encryption protocols.

UNCERTAINTY PRINCIPLE

The more precisely you know position, the less you know momentum — and vice versa.

▾
Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle states that it is fundamentally impossible to know both the exact position and momentum of a particle simultaneously. This isn't a limitation of our instruments — it's a deep truth about nature.

The more precisely we pin down a particle's location, the more uncertain its momentum becomes — and vice versa. The uncertainty is encoded in the formula: Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2.
⚡ KEY FACT This principle explains why electrons don't spiral into atomic nuclei — their position uncertainty creates a minimum energy state that stabilises atoms.
